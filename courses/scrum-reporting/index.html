Scrum & Agile
Fundamentals
Learn how modern software teams organize, plan, and deliver work in short cycles — and how to operate effectively within that rhythm.

Course Overview

Scrum is the most widely used Agile framework in the world. It organizes work into short, focused cycles called Sprints, with clear roles, rituals, and artifacts designed to keep teams aligned and moving. This course teaches you the full Scrum playbook — not just the theory, but what it looks like in practice and how to participate in it effectively as a BA or PM.

Key Concepts Glossary
Sprint
A fixed time-box (usually 1–4 weeks) during which the team creates a usable Increment. Sprints never extend — unfinished work goes back to the backlog.
Velocity
How many story points a team completes per Sprint on average. Used for forecasting, not measuring individual performance.
User Story
A short, human-centered requirement format: "As a [user], I want [action] so that [outcome]." It forces you to anchor features in real user needs.
Definition of Done
A shared checklist that every story must meet before it's considered complete. Prevents "done but not really done" situations.
Backlog Refinement
An ongoing process where the team breaks down, estimates, and clarifies upcoming backlog items. Not a ceremony — a habit.
Empiricism
Scrum's philosophical foundation: make decisions based on what you've actually observed, not on predictions or assumptions.
Practical Exercises
✍️Write 5 User Stories for a product you know
Pick any product (an app, a tool you use at work). Write 5 user stories using the "As a / I want / So that" format. Then add 2–3 acceptance criteria per story. Share with a colleague and see if they understand the intent without explanation.
🗓️Simulate a Sprint Planning session
Take 10 backlog items and "plan" a 2-week Sprint. Decide what fits, what doesn't, and articulate the Sprint Goal. Write it in one sentence that a stakeholder could understand. Notice how hard it is to define a goal that's specific but not just a task list.
🔁Run a personal retrospective
Apply the Retrospective format to a recent project or Sprint at work. Use the "What went well / What didn't / What to change" structure. Write at least one concrete action item with an owner and a deadline — even if both are you.
🎤Practice a Daily Standup — alone
For 5 days in a row, write your standup answers: What did I complete yesterday? What will I do today? What's blocking me? Notice how quickly you identify blockers when you're forced to name them explicitly every day.
📊Build a Product Backlog for a side project
Take a personal or work project idea. Write at least 15 backlog items, then prioritize them using MoSCoW (Must / Should / Could / Won't). Practice grouping them into a logical first Sprint.
🔍Anti-pattern audit
Look at your current team's Scrum practice. Identify 2–3 anti-patterns from Module 5. Write one paragraph on why each happens (the root cause, not the symptom) and what a realistic fix would look like.

Learn to turn raw information into clear, decision-driving communication — whether it's a weekly status update, a dashboard, or a stakeholder report.

Course Overview

Most reports fail before they're read. They bury the key insight in paragraph three, use charts that require a legend to decode, and leave the reader unsure what to do with the information. This course teaches you to design reporting that communicates with intent — the right information, to the right person, in the right format, at the right time.

Key Concepts Glossary
Pyramid Principle
Lead with your conclusion, then support it with evidence. Never bury the key point. Originally from McKinsey, universally applicable.
RAG Status
Red / Amber / Green — a universal health indicator for projects. Red = at risk, Amber = watch this, Green = on track. Simple and powerful.
RAID Log
Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies — a structured tracker that captures everything that could derail the project.
Leading Indicator
A metric that predicts future performance (e.g., number of demos booked → likely future sales). Contrast with lagging indicators that measure past results.
Data-Ink Ratio
Tufte's principle: every pixel in a chart should carry information. Remove gridlines, decorations, and noise. What's left is your insight.
Chartjunk
Visual elements that add complexity without adding information — 3D effects, gradients, excessive labels, decorative images in charts.
Practical Exercises
📝Rewrite a bad status update
Find a status update you've sent or received that didn't clearly communicate project health. Rewrite it using the Pyramid Principle: one-line summary of status, 3 bullets of key info, one clear ask or decision needed. Compare before and after.
📊Chart autopsy
Find 3 charts from reports or presentations you've seen. For each: identify what story it's trying to tell, what chart type was used, and whether it was the right choice. Redraw at least one of them with a different chart type and compare which communicates more clearly.
🗂️Build a mini dashboard
Pick any project or area you track. Define 4–5 metrics that matter. Build a simple dashboard in Google Sheets or Notion. For each metric, write a one-sentence interpretation: "This number is telling us that..." — if you can't write that sentence, the metric doesn't belong there.
🎯Write a sprint report for two different audiences
Take the same set of sprint data. Write a 3-paragraph report for a technical lead. Then write one for a business stakeholder who doesn't know what a sprint is. Notice what you remove, what you add, and how the framing shifts.
🔴Practice the difficult RAG update
Write a status update for a project that's in Amber or Red. Practice being honest without being catastrophic. The goal: the reader understands the risk, trusts you have it in hand, and knows exactly what you need from them.

This course covers human-centered design thinking, emotional intelligence in professional settings, high-quality communication, collaboration tool fluency, and the basics of people management. Key Concepts Glossary
Design Thinking
A human-centered problem-solving methodology. The insight: most failed products solved the wrong problem. Design thinking forces you to validate the problem before solving it.
Psychological Safety
Amy Edmondson's research finding: the #1 predictor of high-performing teams is whether members feel safe to speak up without fear of punishment. It's not niceness — it's candor without consequence.
SBI Feedback
Situation → Behavior → Impact. A structured feedback model: describe the specific situation, the observable behavior (not character), and the concrete impact it had. Keeps feedback factual and discussable.
NVC
Non-Violent Communication (Marshall Rosenberg). Observations → Feelings → Needs → Requests. Separates what happened from how you interpret it, and connects the behavior to an underlying need.
Radical Candor
Kim Scott's framework: challenge people directly while caring about them personally. The opposite of ruinous empathy (nice but ineffective) and obnoxious aggression (honest but unkind).
Jobs to Be Done
A framing for user needs: people don't want a drill — they want a hole. Understanding what "job" a user is "hiring" your product to do leads to better solutions than feature lists.
Practical Exercises
🧠Run a 20-minute design thinking sprint
Pick a small problem someone in your life has (a colleague's frustration, your own workflow inefficiency). Do: 5 min empathy mapping (what do they think/feel/do/say?), 5 min problem statement ("How might we..."), 5 min ideation (at least 6 ideas, no judgment), 5 min picking the strongest idea. Notice how different the solution is when you start from empathy.
🪞EQ self-assessment
Think of 3 recent situations where you reacted in a way you later questioned (got defensive, shut down, overreacted). For each: what triggered you? What did you feel? What did you actually need in that moment? Write the response you wish you'd given. This is the core self-awareness practice.
💬Write the message you've been avoiding
Identify one message you've been putting off sending (a difficult feedback, a boundary-setting email, a "no" you need to give). Write it using the NVC or SBI framework. You don't have to send it. But write it as if you will. Notice where you soften, vague-out, or over-explain — those are the places where you're protecting yourself at the expense of clarity.
🗺️Map your team's collaboration stack
Draw a map of every tool your team uses to communicate, document, and track work. For each tool, write: what it's used for, whether it's the right tool for that job, and whether anyone actually uses it. Identify one tool that's creating noise rather than clarity — and propose what you'd replace it with.
👂Practice active listening in your next meeting
In your next meeting, give yourself one rule: ask twice as many questions as you make statements. After each thing someone says, your only job is to understand — not respond, not solve. Notice what you learn that you would have missed if you'd talked more.
🤝Influence without authority experiment
Identify something you need from a colleague or stakeholder who doesn't report to you. Map: What do they care about? What do they fear? What's in it for them if they help you? Write your ask framed entirely in terms of their interests, not yours. Then send it and observe what happens.
