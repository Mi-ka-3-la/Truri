Many names, one pattern

In accounting this pattern has many names: amortization (assets), recognition (revenue), accruals (expenses). The schedule is the tool that does the math and records the entries automatically.

Every schedule entry is a journal entry — a debit and a credit. At least one of those accounts must be a Balance Sheet account — that is the source being drained.

The rule that makes this accounting

At least one account must be a Balance Sheet account. Without one, there is nothing to drain — the schedule would just move money between two expense buckets with no tracking of what remains.

Three Ways to Calculate Each Entry

The calculation method controls how the total amount is divided into individual period amounts. Choose based on how precise the pro-rating needs to be.

📅
Day

Calculates a daily rate, then multiplies by the exact number of days in each period. Partial months are handled precisely — no rounding shortcuts.

Example
$12,000 ÷ 365 = $32.88/day
Jan (31 days) → $1,019.18
Feb (28 days) → $919.73
Best for partial months
🗓️
Month

Divides the total into equal monthly amounts. If the schedule starts or ends mid-month, those boundary periods are pro-rated by day count. The default method.

Example
$12,000 ÷ 12 = $1,000/month
Jan (full) → $1,000
Feb (full) → $1,000
Default — most common
⚙️
Custom

You define the number of entries and their cadence — monthly, quarterly, or annually. Useful when the payment schedule does not follow standard calendar divisions.

Example
4 quarterly entries
Q1 → $3,000 · Q2 → $3,000
Q3 → $3,000 · Q4 → $3,000
Non-standard schedules

The executor in plain English

The executor is the backend function that does the actual work when the AI calls proposeSchedule. Here it is, directly from avise-api/src/ai/tools/proposeSchedule.ts:

CODE 

const executor: AI.Tools.Executor<ProposeScheduleSchema> =
  async ({ companyId, userId, args }) => {

  const [debitAccount, creditAccount] =
    await Promise.all([
      accountsData.get(companyId, args.debitAccountId),
      accountsData.get(companyId, args.creditAccountId),
    ]);

  const validation =
    validateAccountsIncludeBalanceSheet(
      [debitAccount, creditAccount]
    );

  if (!validation.valid) {
    throw new Error(validation.error);
  }

  const artifact = await createArtifact({
    companyId,
    userId,
    type: "propose_schedule",
    data: { ...args, calculatedEntries }
      as ProposeScheduleDataWithCalculations,
  });

  return { status: "success", artifact };
};
PLAIN ENGLISH

This is the function the backend runs each time the AI calls proposeSchedule. It receives the company, the user, and all the arguments the AI passed.

Promise.all runs both account lookups simultaneously (parallel, not sequential) — so the backend does not waste time waiting for each one before starting the next.

Look up the debit account (e.g. Prepaid Insurance) by its ID.

Look up the credit account (e.g. Insurance Expense) at the same time.

Run validation: check that at least one of the two accounts is a balance sheet account.

If validation fails, throw an error. The AI engine catches this and apologises to the user, explaining which rule was broken.

Create an Artifact record in the database. This is NOT a schedule -- it is a saved proposal. Think of it like a draft email in your outbox.

Store all the parameters the user provided (accounts, amount, dates, dimensions) plus the calculated entries (the 12 monthly rows already computed).

Return success to the AI engine. It will surface the artifact as a preview card in the chat panel.

📬
The draft email analogy

An artifact is exactly like a draft email. The content is composed and saved. Nothing has been sent. You review it, adjust the wording if needed, and then click Send (Accept). Only then does it leave your outbox and actually do something. If you delete the draft, nothing happened.

Why propose and not create?

The tool description says it plainly: "This tool does not create or post anything, it simply offers a suggestion for the user, so it is safe to use without confirming every detail first." That single sentence encodes four important design decisions:

🛡️

Safe to call

The AI can propose a schedule without asking for confirmation first. If the AI misunderstood the request, the accountant simply rejects it. No harm done — nothing has touched the books.

🧑‍💼

Human in the loop

The accountant reviews every entry before anything touches the general ledger. Accounting data requires human sign-off. The AI is an assistant, not the authority.

✏️

Editable before accept

The accountant can adjust dates, amounts, accounts, and dimensions in the review UI before clicking Accept. The proposal is a starting point, not a final answer.

📸

Artifact = snapshot

The artifact stores the full proposal at a single point in time, including pre-calculated entries. If the accountant waits a week before reviewing, the proposal still remembers exactly what was calculated — no recomputation needed.

🏗️
The human-in-the-loop pattern in practice

Any action that modifies financial records — posting journal entries, creating schedules, adjusting balances — goes through the proposal pattern. The AI can prepare as much as it likes. Only a confirmed human click creates real data. This boundary is enforced in the backend: the creation logic only runs when the user explicitly accepts.

Why a join table?

A join table exists when one thing is related to many of another thing. A schedule has many journal entries, and each journal entry belongs to one schedule. The join table (ScheduleLinkedTransaction) holds just two IDs: the schedule ID and the journal entry ID. This lets the database answer two questions efficiently: “Give me all entries for schedule X” and “Which schedule does entry Y belong to?”

The Entry Lifecycle

How journal entries travel from creation to the general ledger — and what thoughtful deletion really means for the accounting record.

Journal Entry Status Flow

↓
Posted
In QuickBooks & the GL. Official record.
Deleted
Soft-archived. Record kept for the audit trail.
✗ hard-deleted

──✗──>
Normal path
Quarantine path — hard delete, no GL impact
Soft-archive — record stays in DB

What Each Status Means

🕐
Scheduled

Created in Avise, waiting to be pushed to QuickBooks. Not yet on the general ledger. Safe to edit or delete without any accounting consequences.

✅
Posted

Pushed to QuickBooks and now part of the official accounting record. It has affected the trial balance. Cannot be undone without an archive (soft delete) — the record must be preserved.

🔒
Quarantined

Blocked from posting — usually because the accounting period is locked or the entry failed a validation rule. Still editable. When the issue is resolved, it transitions back to Scheduled. It has never touched the GL.

🗃️
Deleted

Soft-archived. The entry still exists in the database for audit purposes but is treated as if it no longer exists for all accounting calculations. The audit trail is intact.

Inside the Delete: Sorting Entries into Buckets

When you delete a schedule, the system doesn't treat all its journal entries the same way. It sorts them into three buckets first — then handles each bucket differently. Here is the exact TypeScript that does that sorting.

crud.ts const result = schedule.scheduleLinkedTransactions.reduce( (acc, transaction) => { const { journalEntry } = transaction; const { displayId } = journalEntry; if (journalEntry.status === JournalEntryStatus.Quarantined) { return { ...acc, entriesToHardDelete: acc.entriesToHardDelete .concat(displayId), }; } else if (journalEntry.status === JournalEntryStatus.Deleted) { return { ...acc, entriesThatAreDeleted: acc.entriesThatAreDeleted .concat(displayId), }; } else { return { ...acc, entriesToArchive: acc.entriesToArchive .concat(displayId), }; } }, { entriesToHardDelete: [] as number[], entriesToArchive: [] as number[], entriesThatAreDeleted: [] as number[], }, );
Plain English
Go through every journal entry linked to this schedule.
For each entry, decide what to do with it when the schedule is deleted.
If Quarantined — it has never touched QuickBooks, so we can safely hard-delete it (remove it from the database entirely).
If already Deleted — nothing to do, skip it.
Everything else (Scheduled, Posted) — soft-archive it: change status to Deleted, keep the record.
Start with three empty buckets: entries to hard-delete, entries to soft-archive, entries already deleted.
💬
Comment from the source code — crud.ts
"We are hard deleting Quarantined JEs. Therefore these JEs have never been posted to QBO and have had no effect on the GL. We delete them so they aren't picked up when computing the trial balance for validation of ingest. Otherwise we would have to have a way to know to exclude them."
📌
Why this distinction matters

This is a fundamental accounting data principle. You never destroy records that touched the real financial system — the audit trail must be preserved. But records that never reached the GL — like Quarantined entries — can safely be cleaned up so they don't pollute validation logic.

Recalculation: Two Flavours

What happens when the original amount changes after some entries have already been posted? Avise offers two recalculation strategies depending on how far back the correction needs to go.

⏩
Prospective Recalculate

Recalculates only future (not-yet-posted) entries. Past posted entries stay exactly as they are. Used when the total amount changes partway through the term.

Jan–Mar (posted) unchanged ✓
Apr–Dec (scheduled) recalculated ↻
Safe — no GL impact
⏪
Retrospective Recalculate

Recalculates from the start. All existing entries are replaced: posted entries get archived and reversed. Used when a fundamental error is found that affects the entire schedule.

Jan–Mar (posted) archived + reversed
Apr–Dec (scheduled) recalculated ↻
Serious — touches the GL
⚠️
Retrospective recalculation is serious

It reverses entries that were already on the books. The UI has a confirmation step before allowing this, because it affects closed accounting periods and alters the historical general ledger record.

When Things Go Wrong: The Notification System

Even well-designed systems can have partial failures. When entries fail to archive during a deletion, the system has a layered notification strategy to make sure users always find out.

1
Detect the failure

After attempting to archive entries, the system checks which entry IDs failed. If any failed, it needs to inform the user with enough context to investigate.

2
Check the feature flag

The system reads the in-app-notification flag to decide which notification channel to use.

3a
Flag ON → in-app notification via Knock

Sends a structured in-app notification with the list of failed entry IDs, the company name, and the schedule name — so the user has full context inside the product.

3b
Flag OFF → failure email

Falls back to sending an email to the user with the same context. The old behaviour stays as the reliable fallback while the new notification channel is being rolled out.

Notification Payload Fields

companyName The name of the company whose schedule failed — gives the user immediate context about which account is affected.
companyId The internal company identifier — allows the engineering team to look up the exact record if they need to investigate further.
preparedScheduleName The human-readable name of the schedule that was being deleted — so the user knows exactly which schedule to revisit.
failedEntryIds The list of entry IDs that could not be archived. Precise enough for an accountant or engineer to identify and retry the specific entries that failed.
🔀
Why use a feature flag here?

Feature flags let the team roll out new capabilities (in-app notifications) gradually — enabling them for selected organisations or in staging — without deploying new code or risking the reliability of the existing email fallback. Both paths deliver the same information; the flag controls how it arrives.

AI PDF parsing — how dates appear automatically

When an accountant uploads an invoice PDF, Avise does not make them read it and type the dates manually. The function below extracts the text from the PDF and asks Claude to find the term dates. Here is exactly what the code does, line by line.

CODE 

async function askGptToReadFileAndReturnTermMeta(
  companyId: string,
  fileBuffer: ArrayBufferLike,
): Promise<PreparedScheduleTermMeta> {

  const allTextJoined =
    await pdfParsing.getTextFromFileBuffer(
      fileBuffer
    );

  if (!allTextJoined.match(/.*[0-9]+.*/)) {
    return logAndHandleExpectedError(
      "PDF did not include any numbers."
    );
  }

  const gptResponse =
    await sendConversationMessagesToProvider({
      companyId,
      singleMessageString: allTextJoined,
      systemPrompt: `
        You are a CPA aiding entry to an ERP system.
        You will be provided the text of an invoice,
        and you will respond with the term start and
        end dates in MM/DD/YYYY format. Provide the
        dates delimited by a comma with the term start
        date first. Include no labels or other
        formatting.
      `,
      context: { action: "generateScheduleDates" },
    });

  return getDatesFromGptString(
    gptResponse.content
  );
}
PLAIN ENGLISH

This function takes two inputs: the company ID (for routing the AI call to the right provider config) and the raw bytes of the uploaded PDF file.

Extract all the readable text from the PDF parsing library. The result is a single long string — all the words and numbers from every page joined together.

Safety check: if the extracted text contains zero numbers, it cannot possibly contain a date. Return a graceful fallback error rather than sending useless text to the AI. The accountant will need to enter dates manually.

Send the entire PDF text to Claude (referred to as "gpt" in the variable name — a legacy of the codebase). The text is the user message; the system prompt tells Claude exactly what to do with it.

The system prompt is intentionally terse: act as a CPA, return only two dates in MM/DD/YYYY format separated by a comma, no labels, no other text.

Parse the response from the LLM using getDatesFromGptString. This parser checks for exactly one comma and exactly two date values. If Claude did not follow the format instructions, this returns an expected error and the accountant enters dates manually.

💡
Why such a terse system prompt?

The system prompt says "Include no labels or other formatting." This is intentional. Getting structured output from an LLM requires being extremely prescriptive. The parsing code (getDatesFromGptString) checks for exactly one comma and exactly two date values. If Claude deviates — even with a helpful "Start date: " prefix — the parse fails and the function returns a graceful failure. This is production-grade LLM usage: assume the model can get it wrong, and build a clean fallback for when it does.

Source lines — the financial paper trail

A source line answers the question: "where did this balance come from?" Follow the chain from invoice to zero.

1
Invoice arrives — accountant posts a journal entry to Accounts Payable

DR Prepaid Expense $12,000 / CR Accounts Payable $12,000. The credit line in this entry is a source line. It represents a real obligation the company has — they owe the vendor $12,000.

2
Accountant creates a schedule and links it to that source line

The schedule stores the source line's ID in its sourceLines array. This proves the schedule is backed by a real transaction. It is not a number entered out of thin air — it traces directly to the original invoice entry in the general ledger.

3
Each month, the schedule posts an entry that draws down the balance

DR Insurance Expense $1,000 / CR Prepaid Expense $1,000. This entry is recorded as a ScheduleLinkedTransaction. The Prepaid Expense balance falls from $12,000 toward zero over 12 months.

4
The Roll-Forward Report traces the full journey

Opening balance → monthly entries → closing balance. At the end of the term the balance reaches zero and every entry links back through the schedule to the original source line. An auditor can trace from the final $0 balance all the way back to the original invoice.

TYPE 

type ScheduleWithJournalEntriesAndFullSourceLines = {
  id: string;
  name: string;
  termStart: Date | null;
  termEnd: Date | null;
  dimensionValueId: string | null;
  companyId: string;
  scheduleLinkedTransactions:
    ScheduleLinkedTransaction[];
  sourceLines: FullSourceLine[];
  totalAmount: number | null;
};
PLAIN ENGLISH

This TypeScript type describes the full schedule object that the system loads when you open a schedule detail view. It combines the schedule record itself with all its linked data in one shape.

termStart and termEnd are nullable — they can be null if the accountant has not entered dates yet (or if PDF date extraction was needed but failed).

dimensionValueId is nullable — a schedule can exist without a dimension (no cost-centre tag), though that is uncommon in practice.

scheduleLinkedTransactions — the list of all journal entries this schedule has generated. Each element is a join record pointing to an actual JournalEntry (which may be scheduled, posted, or deleted).

sourceLines — the source lines attached to this schedule. Each is a ScheduleSourceLine joined with its journalLine — the raw accounting line from the original transaction.

totalAmount is the controlling total — the sum of all posted entries must eventually equal this number. It is nullable only while the schedule is still being set up.

The Roll-Forward Report takes all journal entries generated by schedules, groups them by period, and shows three numbers for each period. Here is what it looks like for a $12,000 prepaid insurance schedule.

Period Opening Balance Entries Posted Closing Balance
Jan 2024 $12,000 ($1,000) $11,000
Feb 2024 $11,000 ($1,000) $10,000
Mar 2024 $10,000 ($1,000) $9,000
… Apr – Nov follow the same pattern, $1,000 each month …
Dec 2024 $1,000 ($1,000) $0 ✓
Opening Balance
What was on the books

The balance in the account at the start of the period — i.e. what the previous period's closing balance was.

Entries Posted
What changed

The net total of all journal entries posted by schedules in this period. Shown in parentheses to indicate a reduction of the asset balance.

Closing Balance
What remains

Opening balance plus entries posted. When this reaches $0 the schedule has fully amortised — no balance remains on the books.

📌
Why auditors love this report

Roll-forward reports are a standard accounting deliverable. They prove that a balance sheet account moved in the way it should have, entry by entry. This report is what turns a pile of journal entries into a coherent story about how a prepaid asset or deferred revenue balance changed over time. Without the Roll-Forward Report, you have journal entries — but no story.
